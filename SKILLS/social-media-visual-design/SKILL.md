# Social Media Visual Design

## Purpose

Create social-media visual systems and production-ready concepts that feel intentionally art-directed, legible on a phone, and genuinely native to the brand. Do not substitute a tidy but generic template for a real visual direction.

Use this skill for social carousels, reels covers, posts, story templates, feed systems, visual identity exploration, or Canva directions.

## Core Standard

A visual is successful when it has:

1. **A point of view** — it feels chosen, not assembled from default components.
2. **Human editorial judgment** — optical hierarchy, deliberate imbalance, breathing room, and texture only where meaningful.
3. **Mobile legibility** — the topic, hierarchy, and action are intelligible at feed size.
4. **Brand specificity** — it could plausibly belong to this brand and not any generic creator account.
5. **Production realism** — it can be recreated and varied consistently in the selected design tool.

## Start With a Visual Brief, Not a Palette

Before designing, identify:

- Brand personality and emotional effect
- Audience and viewing context
- Content role: teach, relate, prove, converse, or sell
- Platform and surface: feed, carousel cover, reel cover, story
- Desired behavior: save, share, reply, follow, or inquire
- Existing visual assets and practical production constraints
- Whether the task is exploration, a direction choice, or production

If any of these materially determine the outcome and are unknown, state the smallest missing input. Do not invent brand history, photography, testimonials, or a final approved identity.

## Reference-Led Exploration Protocol

When the user says a concept looks generic, AI-generated, too corporate, too sterile, or insufficiently human:

1. **Acknowledge the exact failure plainly.** For example: formulaic cards, default centered hierarchy, decorative tech marks, unearned gradients, overly uniform spacing, or synthetic stock aesthetics.
2. **Study real references before re-generating.** Use user-provided folders, design libraries, editorial sources, and relevant live references. Extract principles, not copied layouts.
3. **Make directions materially different.** Do not offer three recolors of the same composition. Vary the visual grammar: type-led editorial, annotated notebook, reportage, art-book collage, archival index, etc.
4. **Show an actual representation.** Provide thumbnails, a moodboard, a Canva candidate, or a rendered template. Do not ask the user to choose solely from text descriptions.
5. **Explain the distinction in concrete visual terms.** Describe hierarchy, composition, image treatment, typographic contrast, and repeatable components.
6. **Iterate from the user's critique.** Treat negative feedback as a design constraint to retain, not a one-off preference.

## Anti-Generic Visual Checklist

Reject or rework concepts that rely on several of these at once:

- Centered headline + colored pill + floating icon + three rounded cards
- Generic glossy 3D objects, AI-style illustration, or abstract blobs without semantic value
- Neon gradients or loud accent colors used only to look modern
- Uniform card grids used for every content type
- Decorative labels, badges, pagination, or pseudo-dashboard details with no reader purpose
- Excessive dividers, boxed sections, or corner UI ornaments
- Perfectly symmetrical layouts when the content asks for editorial tension
- Overloaded text with no typographic hierarchy
- Stock-photo smiles or staged study scenes with no documentary credibility
- A design whose main identity can be swapped for any other brand in 30 seconds

## Human Editorial Design Principles

### Typography

- Let typography carry the visual identity before adding decoration.
- Use a deliberate contrast: expressive but readable serif/display face for selective emphasis, paired with a functional sans serif for information.
- Use scale, weight, leading, line breaks, and alignment to create hierarchy. Do not compensate for weak hierarchy with lots of colors or shapes.
- Check line breaks manually. Never leave a headline broken at an awkward word purely because a template chose it.

### Layout

- Prefer optical alignment over mathematical centering.
- Use asymmetry intentionally: offset text blocks, a cropped image, a margin that creates pause, or a detail that anchors the page.
- Build one dominant reading path. A carousel cover should have a clear first read in under two seconds.
- Leave real negative space. Empty area can be an editorial device, not unfinished work.
- Design a system with a few layout families, not one rigid template replicated indefinitely.

### Surface and Imagery

- Use material cues sparingly and purposefully: scanned paper, annotation marks, marginalia, documentary photography, lab or desk details, archival snippets, real diagrams.
- When creating a light/white background, use pure crisp white or faint off-white with low-opacity structural texture (e.g. subtle mathematical/architectural grid, faint paper grain) to give tactile presence without visual noise.
- Apply natural multi-layered soft drop shadows (diffuse ambient shadow with low opacity) under elevated cards and buttons so elements feel like tactile physical paper sheets/slabs rather than flat, stiff vector boxes.
- Avoid clashing neon colors when designing authoritative content. Favor a disciplined, high-contrast dominant tone (e.g., Deep Navy `#0A192F` / `#0B192C`) paired with neutral cards and at most one purposeful warm accent for key CTAs.
- When leveraging geometric type systems like Montserrat, exploit the full family range (Light vs ExtraBold) for sharp hierarchical tension rather than introducing decorative shapes to compensate.
- If imagery is unavailable, use typography and information design rather than inventing synthetic scenes.
- Any real client evidence, data, or research image requires permission and source checking.

### Color

- Start with a restrained base and one purposeful accent.
- Use color to group, signal, emphasize, or navigate, not merely decorate.
- Avoid pure black/white extremes when a softer, more intentional editorial neutral serves the brand better.
- Test contrast and readability on a phone-sized preview.

## Monogram & Single-Letter Brandmark Design

When designing a single-letter logo/monogram (e.g. "N" mark) for modern editorial/academic brands:

1. **Avoid Generic Letterforms**: A lettermark is not just a font glyph in a colored circle. It must embody a conceptual mechanism (e.g., negative space cutout, page fold, margin bracket, crossover ribbon, or editorial stencil).
2. **Distinct Visual Grammars in Exploration**: When exploring options, do not provide slight variations of the same geometric pill/block. Provide fundamentally distinct structural routes:
   - *Page-Cut / Dog-Ear Signature*: Bold letter silhouette with an editorial corner cut/fold.
   - *Margin Rule / Annotation System*: Letter framed by manuscript margin lines and citation brackets.
   - *Continuous Monoline / Ribbon*: Single-stroke geometry with optical crossovers.
   - *Negative Space Window / Tile*: Letter carved out of a container shape (ideal for app icons and square avatars).
3. **Small-Scale Avatar Testing (96px/32px)**: Always test marks at thumbnail sizes before finalizing. Reject marks with fragile hairlines or over-fractured stencil cuts that collapse at favicon/avatar scale.
4. **Restrained Color Application**: Use the mark in monochrome (single tone) or at most two tones (base structure + single purposeful accent on the key conceptual detail, such as the page fold or margin rule).

## Direction Presentation Format

For a brand visual exploration, present 3–4 directions using this structure:

```text
DIRECTION NAME
Emotional territory:
Visual grammar:
Typography:
Palette role (not merely hex codes):
Image/material treatment:
Best content roles:
What makes it distinct:
What it deliberately avoids:
Production feasibility:
```

Then create a visual representation for each direction. The examples must use different layouts and content roles where possible, rather than changing only colors.

## From Direction to Template System

Only after a direction is chosen or a hybrid is clearly requested, create a lightweight system:

1. **Carousel cover** — strongest visual hook, topic/series cue, not a cluttered title page.
2. **Educational body slide** — information hierarchy, one key idea per slide.
3. **Checklist / framework slide** — supports actionable content without dashboard cliché.
4. **Relatable/opinion post** — more expressive, text-led, native to informal content.
5. **Proof or offer post** — restrained; truth and clarity outrank conversion pressure.
6. **Story/reel cover** — recognizable in a small crop.

For each template define: safe margin, text hierarchy, image area rules, accent use, footer/signature policy, and content categories it should not be used for.

## Canva Workflow

- Treat AI-generated Canva candidates as **exploration**, not final art direction.
- Use a detailed brief: brand personality, audience, exact content role, constraints, typography mood, composition, material treatment, banned clichés, and desired emotional outcome.
- Generate a small set of candidates, then select and edit the strongest one instead of accepting the first output.
- **Canva URL & Login Wall Protocol**: Canva `/edit` URLs require active browser login sessions and will fail with auth redirects in automated browser tools or headless API fetchers. When collaborating with users on Canva designs:
  - If sharing an editable baseline, provide both the edit URL and view URL.
  - When inspecting user edits from Canva, explicitly request an exported image (JPG/PNG) or a public view link (`"Anyone with the link can view"`), rather than relying on browser navigation to private `/edit` links.
- **Canva MCP & WebGL DOM Limitations**:
  - Canva uses WebGL / custom HTML5 canvas rendering for slide content; text inside the canvas cannot be selected or modified via standard DOM text input injection or browser keyboard simulation into canvas buffers.
  - If Canva MCP edit transactions experience timeouts or auth desync, avoid getting stuck in tool-retry loops or pretending DOM input modified the visual canvas.
  - **Operator Execution & Hand-off Policy**: When the user expects the agent to execute edits ("loh nggk kmu yg kerjain?"), clearly communicate technical boundaries (WebGL rendering prevents synthetic typing on canvas without working MCP edit transactions). Bridge this immediately by supplying a structured 1:1 **Slide-by-Slide Copy & Asset Matrix** mapped directly to the template's visual slots (e.g. Stencil Cover Hook, Index `00`-`05`, Cutout Photo Prompts, and Ending CTA) alongside platform-native captions and companion posts.
  - **Template Information Density Check**: Watch for user feedback on information density (e.g. switching templates because "terlalu sedikit informasi yang di share"). Academic/educational carousels require substantive, high-utility conceptual frameworks (e.g. operational definitions, statistical choice rules) rather than sparse, single-sentence lifestyle/habit slides.
  - **High-Impact Editorial Carousel Pattern (Tested)**:
    1. *Cover Slide (Warm Neutral)*: Ultra-Bold left-aligned hook + imperfect marker oval/circle encircling the single emotional trigger keyword (e.g. "DITOLAK", "REVISI") + clear pill CTA (`[ Swipe buat bedah ]`).
    2. *Pattern Interrupt Slide (Vivid Accent Color, e.g. Solid Orange/Terracotta)*: High-contrast typography with logic-equation pills (`[ variabel jelas = ]` `[ uji statistik aman ]` -> `[ lebih siap sempro ]`).
    3. *Deep Tone Slide (Midnight Navy / Dark Slate)*: Contrast slide with white body copy and vivid orange highlighted core question.
    4. *Callout / Quote Slide*: Rounded rectangular callout container housing critical advisor/examiner questions (e.g. *"Sudah uji validitas?"*).
    5. *Action / Soft-Selling CTA Slide*: Clean recap headline with marker highlight + save bookmark pill button + subtle non-aggressive footer support copy.

### Strict Carousel Layout & Header/Footer Rules (User Enforced)
- **Top-Left Quadrant**: Strictly place the small official single-letter mark (e.g. 'N' mark) with NO text beside it and NO decorative icons (ban random starbursts, sparkle stars, or floating geometric glyphs in the top bar).
- **Background Badging on Dark/Vivid Slides**: DO NOT wrap the logo in an awkward background box/badge on dark navy or vivid orange slides. The logo must sit transparently on the canvas; adapt the logo mark's strokes/fills if needed for high contrast.
- **Top-Right & Metadata Hygiene**: Ban pagination artifacts in the body canvas (e.g. accidental template strings like "page two", "page four", "next page").
- **Footer Simplicity**: Maintain a clean, two-element footer — handle/signature on the bottom-left (`naskah.fk`), bare slide counter on the bottom-right (`01/05`).
- **Whitespace & Margin Consistency**: Maintain strict uniform margins across all slides (e.g., 90px left/right margins) and ensure vertical gaps between headline, body copy, and container pills feel balanced rather than arbitrarily shifted.
- Never imply a candidate has been added to the user's Canva account, finalized, scheduled, or approved unless that status has been verified.
- Keep the final working layout reproducible with Canva fonts, components, and assets.

## Social Content Guardrails

- Design must strengthen the underlying message; a pretty template cannot rescue vague content.
- Educational visuals prioritize scanability and saves.
- Relatable visuals prioritize recognition and emotional rhythm.
- Offer visuals must remain calm and explanatory, not price-led unless a specific campaign calls for it.
- Use one primary CTA at most; visual clutter is an anti-CTA.

## Final QC

Before presenting a visual or template, check:

- Does it look intentional at 25% zoom / feed size?
- Is the first reading path obvious within two seconds?
- Is the type readable and does it have a meaningful hierarchy?
- Are any design elements merely AI decoration?
- Does it feel specific to the brand rather than a generic study account?
- Does it suit the content role and platform surface?
- Is it feasible to reproduce consistently?
- Is it safe, truthful, and free of invented proof?
